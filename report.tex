\section{Introduction}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Credit card default prediction is a binary classification problem with a
pronounced class imbalance: most customers do not default. The UCI Taiwan
dataset records repayment history for 30,000 customers from April to
September 2005, and the target is whether each customer defaulted on their
October 2005 payment. Of the 30,000 original records, 6,636 (22.1\%)
defaulted. This 78/22 split makes accuracy an unreliable primary metric
and forces evaluation to focus on precision-recall behaviour.

This report documents a team experiment comparing four model families on
the same dataset, train/test split, and evaluation protocol. The fixed
pipeline constraint for the main model (Contributor~1) requires a Keras
dense autoencoder as a feature extractor feeding an XGBoost classifier.
Three teammates independently implemented alternative pipelines:
Logistic Regression with and without PCA dimensionality reduction
(Contributor~2), UMAP nonlinear projection with k-nearest neighbours
(Contributor~3), and a radial-basis-function SVM with and without
Linear Discriminant Analysis (Contributor~4).

Each pipeline tests whether the chosen dimensionality reduction technique
helps or hurts, and what class-imbalance handling strategy performs best.
Section~\ref{sec:results} consolidates all results under a shared evaluation
protocol so the models are directly comparable.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Dataset}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The raw CSV contains 25 columns. One is a customer identifier (dropped before
modelling). Three are demographics: AGE, SEX (1 = male, 2 = female), and two
categoricals with documented codes: EDUCATION (1 = graduate school,
2 = university, 3 = high school, 4 = others) and MARRIAGE (1 = married,
2 = single, 3 = others).

The remaining 19 columns record six months of payment history for each
customer. For each month from April (subscript~6) to September (subscript~1)
the dataset records: the repayment status (\texttt{PAY\_0}--\texttt{PAY\_6},
where $-2$ and $-1$ indicate early or on-time payment, 1--8 indicate months
of delay), the statement balance (\texttt{BILL\_AMT1}--\texttt{BILL\_AMT6},
in New Taiwan dollars), and the amount paid (\texttt{PAY\_AMT1}--\texttt{PAY\_AMT6},
in NT\$). Credit limits (\texttt{LIMIT\_BAL}) range from 10,000 to
1,000,000~NT\$.

The dataset has three known quality issues. The EDUCATION column contains
codes 0, 5, and 6 that the dataset README does not define; 468 rows carry
these codes. The MARRIAGE column contains code~0 in 54 rows, also
undocumented. Both are recoded as described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.
Additionally, 35 rows are exact duplicates of other rows, which must be
removed before splitting to prevent test-set leakage. After removal,
29,965 rows remain.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Preprocessing and Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Data hygiene}

Three hygiene operations run before any feature engineering. EDUCATION codes
0, 5, and 6 are recoded to 4 (``others''), consistent with the README's
category for ambiguous responses. MARRIAGE code~0 appears in 54 rows; it is
recoded to 3 (``others'') by the same logic. Then 35 exact duplicate rows are
dropped and the index is reset, leaving 29,965 rows.

\subsection{Feature engineering}

Feeding 18 raw temporal columns directly to any model has two costs: it
multiplies the feature count and introduces strong within-group
multicollinearity. The six BILL\_AMT columns, for instance, track a single
underlying credit exposure and correlate with one another above $r = 0.9$.
Summary statistics capture the same information at a fraction of the dimension.

From the six repayment-status columns we compute three aggregates:
\texttt{Pay\_delay\_mean} (average months of delay across the six months),
\texttt{Pay\_delay\_max} (worst single month), and
\texttt{Pay\_delay\_std} (volatility of the delay pattern). From the six
bill amounts we compute \texttt{Bill\_mean}, \texttt{Bill\_std}, and
\texttt{Bill\_max}. From the six payment amounts we compute
\texttt{Pays\_amts\_total} and \texttt{Pays\_amts\_mean}. We also add
\texttt{Utilization\_1} (September bill divided by credit limit),
\texttt{Utilization\_mean} (six-month average utilization),
\texttt{n\_months\_over\_limit} (count of months where the balance
exceeded the credit limit, ranging 0--6), and a composite
\texttt{Risk\_score} defined as
$\texttt{Pay\_delay\_mean} + \texttt{Utilization\_mean}
+ \texttt{Bill\_mean} / (\texttt{LIMIT\_BAL} + 1)$.

After engineering, all 18 raw temporal columns except \texttt{PAY\_0} are
dropped. \texttt{PAY\_0} records September 2005 repayment status, is not
summarised by the engineered features (it is the most recent value, not an
average), and has the second-highest Pearson correlation with the target
at $r = 0.325$.

Three engineered columns (\texttt{Pays\_amts\_total}, \texttt{Utilization\_1},
and \texttt{Risk\_score}) are kept in the saved CSV for interpretability but
excluded from training. \texttt{Pays\_amts\_total} equals six times
\texttt{Pays\_amts\_mean}, creating a perfect linear dependency.
\texttt{Utilization\_1} covers a single month, already summarised by
\texttt{Utilization\_mean}. \texttt{Risk\_score} is a linear combination
of features already in the training set. The final training feature matrix
has 18 columns.

\subsection{Log transformation of heavy-tailed monetary features}

The autoencoder trains under mean squared error (MSE) loss; standard scaler
normalisation precedes it. Before transformation, the monetary columns are
severely right-skewed: individual bill amounts reach 964,511~NT\$, and after
StandardScaler one training row reached $|z| = 72.9$ on a bill column. A
single such row dominates the gradient and forces the network to waste
capacity reconstructing extreme rows.

We apply a signed log-plus-one transform,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{x} = \operatorname{sign}(x) \cdot \log(1 + |x|),
\end{equation}
to six columns: \texttt{LIMIT\_BAL}, \texttt{Bill\_mean}, \texttt{Bill\_std},
\texttt{Bill\_max}, \texttt{Pays\_amts\_mean}, and \texttt{Utilization\_mean}.
The signed form preserves the direction of negative bill values, which arise
from refunds and occur in approximately 2\% of rows. After transformation and
StandardScaler, the maximum $|z|$ across all features drops to the range
5--8. The same transformation is applied when the data is used by the
logistic regression, KNN, and SVM pipelines, so all four models operate on
identically preprocessed features.

\subsection{Categorical encoding}

SEX, EDUCATION, and MARRIAGE are nominal variables. Treating them as
ordinal integers and computing MSE on them (as the autoencoder would) imposes
a false distance structure: predicting MARRIAGE $= 2.3$ when the true code
is~2 appears as a small error even though the two are semantically unrelated.
We apply one-hot encoding with \texttt{drop\_first=True} to avoid perfect
multicollinearity, adding a net four binary columns (one for SEX, two for
EDUCATION, one for MARRIAGE). \texttt{PAY\_0} is left as a numeric integer
because its values are genuinely ordinal: higher numbers correspond to longer
payment delays.

\subsection{Train, calibration, and test splits}

All four model families use the same three-way split. The 29,965-row dataset
is split 80/20 by stratified random sampling with seed~42, giving a training
set of 23,972 rows and a test set of 5,993 rows. Both retain the 22.1\%
default rate. A 10\% calibration slice (2,397 rows, stratified) is carved
from the training set and held out from model fitting entirely. Its sole use
is post-hoc probability calibration and threshold selection. After removing
the calibration slice, the effective training set is 21,575 rows.

For experiments using SMOTE-based oversampling, the balanced file
\texttt{train\_balanced.csv} (37,336 rows, 50\% default rate) was generated
by applying SMOTENC to the original training split. SMOTENC, rather than
plain SMOTE, is used because the one-hot dummy columns (SEX\_2,
EDUCATION\_2, EDUCATION\_3, EDUCATION\_4, MARRIAGE\_2, MARRIAGE\_3) are
binary categoricals; interpolating them as continuous values would produce
fractional dummies such as SEX\_2~$= 0.3$. SMOTENC keeps categorical columns
at valid binary values during oversampling. The test set and calibration slice
are never resampled.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All four model families use an identical evaluation protocol to ensure the
results are comparable.

\subsection{PR-AUC as the primary metric}

The precision-recall (PR) curve plots precision
($TP / (TP + FP)$) against recall ($TP / (TP + FN)$) as the decision
threshold varies. PR-AUC is the area under this curve. For a dataset with
22.1\% positive rate, a classifier that randomly ranks examples achieves
PR-AUC $\approx 0.221$; a perfect ranker achieves 1.0.

We prefer PR-AUC over ROC-AUC because the ROC curve is built from the true
positive rate and the false positive rate, and the false positive rate is
diluted by the large pool of true negatives in an imbalanced dataset. A model
that flags 10\% of the 77.9\% majority class as false positives accumulates
many misclassified customers, yet its false positive rate ($FP / (FP+TN)$)
remains small because the denominator (all non-defaulters) is large. PR-AUC
exposes this by measuring precision directly.

\subsection{Brier score}

The Brier score is the mean squared error of predicted probabilities:
\[
  \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (p_i - y_i)^2.
\]
A model that always predicts the base rate ($p_i = 0.221$) achieves
Brier $= 0.221 \times 0.779 = 0.172$. Our models score between 0.135 and
0.200, where lower values indicate better-calibrated probabilities.
Brier score requires well-calibrated outputs; without post-hoc calibration
the scores would reflect the raw skewed distributions produced by
class-imbalance corrections.

\subsection{Calibration and threshold selection}

All four pipelines apply the same two-step post-processing. First, isotonic
regression calibration (\texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV(method='isotonic',
cv='prefit')}) is fitted on the held-out calibration slice. Isotonic
regression fits a non-decreasing step function mapping raw model scores to
empirical probabilities on the calibration data. Second, the F1-optimal
threshold is found on the calibration slice:
\[
  \tau^* = \arg\max_\tau \, F1(\tau, \text{calibration set}).
\]
This threshold is then applied to the test set. Using the calibration slice
for threshold selection prevents test-set leakage.

For a 22\%-positive dataset the F1-optimal threshold is typically well below
0.5: the model needs to flag more positives to recover enough true positives
to push recall up, at the cost of some precision. All conditions in this
study find $\tau^*$ in the range 0.15--0.71, with the unbalanced conditions
clustering around 0.15--0.38.

\section{Model Architectures and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Autoencoder + XGBoost}

\subsubsection*{Autoencoder architecture and training}

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to reproduce its own input at
the output layer through a low-dimensional bottleneck. The encoder
compresses the input; the decoder reconstructs it. Because the network
cannot copy the input through a narrower channel without losing information,
the bottleneck neurons learn to retain the information most relevant for
reconstruction. Those bottleneck activations, called the latent
representation, replace the original features as input to XGBoost.

The architecture used here maps $18 \to 24 \to 16 \to 10 \to 16 \to 24 \to 18$,
with ReLU activations on all hidden layers, Dropout (rate 0.2) after each
encoder hidden layer, and a linear activation on the output layer. The output
is linear because StandardScaler outputs are real-valued, not bounded to
$[0,1]$. The network is trained with Adam and MSE loss, with early stopping
on the validation loss (patience~$= 5$, restoring best weights).

Two design modifications improve the default class-blind training setup.
\emph{Weighted AE loss} (Condition B.4): because 78\% of training rows are
non-defaulters, a class-blind autoencoder allocates most of its reconstruction
capacity to the majority class. We pass \texttt{sample\_weight} to
\texttt{autoencoder.fit}, upweighting each defaulter row by the inverse class
ratio $N_{\text{non-default}}/N_{\text{default}} \approx 3.52$. The ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:ae_ablation}) shows this changes PR-AUC by only 0.0018,
which is within measurement noise at three seeds. \emph{PAY\_0 concatenation}
(Condition C.3): after encoding, the scaled raw value of \texttt{PAY\_0} is
concatenated to the 10-dimensional latent vector, giving XGBoost an
11-dimensional input. The ablation shows this matters more: removing it
reduces PR-AUC by 0.0094.

\subsubsection*{XGBoost configuration}

XGBoost builds a sequence of shallow decision trees where each tree corrects
the residuals of the previous ensemble. The final prediction is the sum of
all trees' outputs passed through a logistic function.

For the imbalance, XGBoost's \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} multiplies the
gradient contribution of each positive-class example by a factor $w$. We
set $w = 3.52$. Hyperparameter tuning uses a 16-cell grid (2 choices each
for number of estimators, tree depth, learning rate, and minimum child
weight) evaluated by 5-fold stratified cross-validation scored on
average precision. The grid consistently selects \texttt{max\_depth}~$= 4$,
\texttt{learning\_rate}~$= 0.05$, and \texttt{n\_estimators}~$= 200$ across
nearly all conditions and seeds.

\subsection{Logistic Regression with PCA}

Logistic regression fits a linear decision boundary by learning one weight
per feature. The model predicts $P(\text{default}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T
\mathbf{x} + b)$ where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Because this
is a linear model, feature coefficients are directly interpretable: a
positive coefficient on \texttt{Pay\_delay\_mean} means higher average
delay increases the log-odds of default by that coefficient per unit of
the standardised feature.

Logistic regression naturally produces calibrated probabilities through
its link function, so the Brier score is meaningful without additional
isotonic calibration. The same threshold-tuning protocol applies.

PCA (principal component analysis) transforms the standardised features
into orthogonal directions of maximum variance. We retain the components
needed to explain 95\% of the total variance, which reduces 18 features
to 13 principal components. PCA does not use label information; it is
fitted on the training set and applied to calibration and test sets.

Six conditions are tested: original features vs.\ PCA, each crossed with
three class-imbalance strategies (no adjustment, \texttt{class\_weight=
'balanced'}, and SMOTENC oversampling).

\subsection{UMAP + k-Nearest Neighbours}

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) is a nonlinear
dimensionality reduction algorithm. Unlike PCA, which finds directions of
maximum variance in the original feature space, UMAP constructs a
weighted graph capturing the topology of the data's neighbourhood structure
and then finds a low-dimensional layout that preserves that topology.
Concretely, UMAP builds a fuzzy simplicial set representation based on local
distances (controlled by \texttt{n\_neighbors}, which sets the neighbourhood
size) and minimises the cross-entropy between high-dimensional and
low-dimensional fuzzy sets.

We project to 2 dimensions with \texttt{n\_neighbors}~$= 15$ and
\texttt{min\_dist}~$= 0.1$ using Euclidean distance. The 2D projection is
used both for classification (as input to KNN) and for visualisation
(Figures~\ref{fig:umap_class}--\ref{fig:umap_compare}).

k-Nearest Neighbours classifies a test point by majority vote among its
$k$ nearest training neighbours. The predicted probability is the fraction
of the $k$ neighbours that belong to the positive class. Hyperparameters
are tuned by 3-fold stratified cross-validation over $k \in \{5, 11, 15, 21,
31\}$, weights in \{uniform, distance\}, and distance metric in
\{Euclidean, Manhattan\}, scored on F1.

Four conditions are tested: original 18 features vs.\ 2D UMAP, each with
and without SMOTENC oversampling.

\subsection{SVM with LDA}

A support vector machine with an RBF kernel finds a decision boundary that
maximises the margin between classes in a kernel-induced feature space. The
RBF kernel implicitly maps the input into a high-dimensional space where
classes that are not linearly separable in 18D may become separable. We
use \texttt{SVC(kernel='rbf', probability=True, C=1.0, gamma='scale')},
which enables probability outputs through Platt scaling (fitting a logistic
regression on the SVM decision values on a held-out subset of training data).
No hyperparameter search is performed; $C = 1$ and \texttt{gamma='scale'}
(set to $1/(n\_\text{features} \times \text{Var}(\mathbf{X}))$) are used
directly.

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) finds the projection direction that
maximises the ratio of between-class variance to within-class variance. For
binary classification, LDA reduces to a single discriminant direction,
projecting 18 features to 1 scalar. This is the most aggressive compression
tested in the study: 18 features collapsed to 1.

Four conditions are tested: original 18 features vs.\ 1D LDA projection,
each with and without SMOTENC oversampling.

\paragraph{XGB-only vs.\ AE+XGB.}
C0 (PR-AUC~$= 0.5281$) and C1 (0.5280) both outperform every AE variant.
The best AE condition, C2, reaches 0.5217, a gap of 0.006 below C0. This
gap is small but consistent across all three seeds. The autoencoder is not
improving the feature representation on this dataset. The probable cause is
that the 18-feature dataset's monetary columns are highly collinear ($r > 0.9$
within the bill-amount group), leaving little non-linear structure for the
encoder to discover. A direct test confirms this: a 2-neuron bottleneck
achieves near-identical ROC-AUC to the 10-neuron bottleneck, which means
the autoencoder is performing the equivalent of PCA-style linear projection,
not learning a useful non-linear manifold.

\paragraph{PAY\_0 concatenation.}
C4 (latents only, no PAY\_0) achieves PR-AUC~$= 0.5123$, the weakest AE
variant, 0.0094 below C2 (0.5217). The bottleneck of 10 neurons compressing
18 features loses \texttt{PAY\_0}'s signal ($r = 0.325$ with target). Bypassing
the bottleneck by concatenating the raw scaled \texttt{PAY\_0} value directly
to the latent vector recovers 0.0094 PR-AUC. This is the most concrete
design decision the ablation validates.

\paragraph{Weighted AE loss.}
C2 (weighted AE, PR-AUC~$= 0.5217$) vs.\ C3 (class-blind AE, 0.5235) shows
a difference of 0.0018 in favour of the class-blind AE. This is less than the
standard deviation of either condition and reverses direction between conditions,
indicating it is noise. Weighted AE loss has no measurable effect at this dataset
size and seed count.

\paragraph{Latent-space oversampling.}
SMOTE on latents (C5, PR-AUC~$= 0.5177$) beats C4 (latents only, 0.5123)
by 0.0054 but remains below C0 and C2. Borderline-SMOTE (C6, 0.4933)
is the worst condition overall. Borderline-SMOTE restricts synthetic sample
generation to latent vectors near the decision boundary. In a low-variance
10-dimensional latent space, boundary points from both classes are close
together, so the synthetic samples are noisy rather than informative.
Borderline-SMOTE costs 54 seconds of training versus 31 for the non-oversampling
conditions, with a PR-AUC penalty of 0.035 relative to C0.

\paragraph{scale\_pos\_weight sensitivity.}
C0 ($w = 3.52$, PR-AUC~$= 0.5281$) vs.\ C1 ($w = 1$, 0.5280) differ by
0.0001 on PR-AUC and 0.0001 on Brier. XGBoost on raw features with 5-fold
cross-validation scored on average precision does not need the explicit
class weight; the scoring criterion already prioritises the minority class.

\subsection{Effect of oversampling on calibration quality}

Across all four model families, SMOTE consistently raises the Brier score
(worsens calibration) and reduces PR-AUC:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LR}: original Brier~$= 0.1423$, smote Brier~$= 0.1937$ ($+0.051$).
        PR-AUC drops from 0.5087 to 0.4870 ($-0.022$).
  \item \textbf{KNN}: original Brier~$= 0.1429$, smote Brier~$= 0.1722$ ($+0.029$).
        PR-AUC drops from 0.4999 to 0.4749 ($-0.025$). The smote\_UMAP condition
        reaches Brier~$= 0.1977$ ($+0.054$).
  \item \textbf{SVM}: original Brier~$= 0.1418$, balanced Brier~$= 0.1718$ ($+0.030$).
        PR-AUC drops from 0.5144 to 0.4730 ($-0.041$).
  \item \textbf{AE + XGB}: SMOTE on latents (C5) Brier~$= 0.1372$ vs.\ C4 (no SMOTE)
        Brier~$= 0.1382$; the effect is smaller because SMOTE here is applied
        in 10-dimensional latent space rather than the original feature space.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is the same across all families. Training on a balanced 50/50
dataset trains the model to output probabilities near 0.5 on balanced data.
When the model is evaluated on the natural 22\%-positive test distribution,
probabilities are skewed too high for the positive class: the model has
learned that positives occur half the time, but on the test set they occur
only 22\% of the time. Isotonic regression calibration on the calibration
slice corrects some of this, but the isotonic fitter has only 2,397
calibration points and cannot fully correct a large distributional mismatch.
The Brier score increase is the residual of this miscalibration after the
isotonic correction.

\subsection{Effect of dimensionality reduction}

Three dimensionality reduction methods are tested, with very different outcomes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PCA (95\% variance, 18D to 13D, LR)}: PR-AUC loss of 0.006
        (0.5087 to 0.5031). PCA retains 95\% of variance by construction,
        so minimal discriminative information is discarded. For a linear model
        like logistic regression, PCA-orthogonalised features and original
        correlated features carry exactly the same linear information. The
        0.006 drop may reflect the 5\% variance discarded or minor numerical
        differences in the optimisation path.

  \item \textbf{UMAP (18D to 2D, KNN)}: PR-AUC loss of 0.012 (0.4999 to
        0.4879). Compressing to 2 dimensions discards more than PCA, and
        the neighbourhood-preserving objective of UMAP does not guarantee that
        discriminative structure is preserved in 2D when classes overlap in
        higher dimensions. As Figure~\ref{fig:umap_class}(a) shows, the 2D
        embedding has heavy class overlap, which a KNN classifier cannot resolve.

  \item \textbf{LDA (18D to 1D, SVM)}: PR-AUC loss of 0.047 (0.5144 to
        0.4671). Projecting 18 features to a single scalar is the most
        aggressive compression in this study. LDA finds the optimal linear
        separator in 1D, but as Figure~\ref{fig:lda_dist} shows, the
        resulting class distributions heavily overlap even on that optimal
        direction. The RBF-SVM can exploit non-linear interactions in 18D;
        on a 1D scalar it reduces to a threshold classifier.
\end{itemize}

The pattern is: larger dimensionality reduction causes larger PR-AUC loss.
PCA loses 33\% of the features and 0.006 PR-AUC; UMAP loses 89\% and
0.012 PR-AUC; LDA loses 94\% and 0.047 PR-AUC. Dimensionality reduction
is not free.

\subsection{ROC-AUC vs.\ PR-AUC divergence}

The SVM presents the clearest example of ROC-AUC and PR-AUC diverging.
SVM original achieves PR-AUC~$= 0.5144$ but ROC-AUC~$= 0.7127$, lower than
both KNN original (ROC-AUC~$= 0.7502$) and LR original (ROC-AUC~$= 0.7458$).

This divergence means the SVM ranks the highest-confidence positives well
(the top of its score distribution contains a dense cluster of true defaulters)
but its score distribution for the rest of the positive class interleaves with
the negative class more than LR or KNN does. PR-AUC weights precision at
every recall level equally, so a model that is very precise at low recall
(top of the ranked list) scores well even if it degrades at higher recall.
ROC-AUC weights true positive rate vs.\ false positive rate at every threshold,
treating all threshold levels symmetrically, so the SVM's weaker performance
at the tail of its distribution costs ROC-AUC more than it costs PR-AUC.

At a 22\% positive rate, PR-AUC is the more meaningful metric for the
deployment scenario of flagging a manageable subset of accounts for review.
A credit risk team will review the top-$k$ flagged accounts rather than
all accounts above a fixed threshold; for that use case, high precision at
low recall is what matters, and PR-AUC measures it.

Unbalanced conditions cluster between 0.152 and 0.336. The SVM's low

of this collapse, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Three seeds are insufficient to rank close conditions.}
Standard deviation estimates from three samples carry approximately 50\%
relative error. Four of the seven AE+XGB conditions lie within one standard
deviation of the top PR-AUC. No reliable ordering is possible among C0, C1,
C2, and C3 from this data alone. Seven or more seeds would be needed.

\paragraph{The autoencoder is over-parameterised for this dataset.}
A 2-neuron bottleneck achieves near-identical AUC to the 10-neuron one.
The effective intrinsic dimensionality of the data is well below 10, consistent
with the high multicollinearity in the bill-amount features. An autoencoder
added to a problem where the data lies near a low-dimensional linear subspace
performs PCA-equivalent compression without the non-linear benefit that
motivates using it.

\paragraph{SVM and LR used fixed hyperparameters.}
Only KNN was grid-searched. The SVM was run with $C = 1$ and default
\texttt{gamma='scale'} without exploring other values. Logistic regression
used the default \texttt{C = 1}. The XGB-only conditions were grid-searched
across 16 cells with 5-fold CV. This asymmetry means the SVM and LR results
may understate their best achievable performance, while XGB results are
closer to their optimum.

\paragraph{SMOTE applied in full feature space for LR, KNN, and SVM.}
The preprocessed features include one-hot dummies; plain SMOTE would produce
fractional binary values. SMOTENC is used instead and correctly preserves
integer dummy values. However, SMOTENC synthetic samples are generated from
all 18 features jointly, which may not respect the correlational structure
of the original data as well as a method that treats continuous and binary
features separately throughout oversampling.

\paragraph{Temporal data is treated as cross-sectional.}
Each row is one customer's 6-month history condensed to summary statistics.
No sequential structure is modelled within or across customers. The dataset
covers a single calendar period (April--September 2005 history, October 2005
target) and cannot be used to study concept drift or seasonal effects.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Four model families were applied to the same 29,965-row preprocessing
output, train/test split, and evaluation protocol. The results are concrete:
XGBoost on 18 raw features (PR-AUC~$= 0.5281$) outperforms every other
configuration tested. The AE+XGB pipeline (PR-AUC~$= 0.5217$) is second,
followed by SVM (0.5144), logistic regression (0.5087), and KNN (0.4999).

Three findings hold across all four model families. First, the original
class distribution (no SMOTE) consistently outperforms oversampled variants
on both PR-AUC and Brier score. SMOTE raises the Brier score by 0.030--0.054
depending on the model and degrades PR-AUC by 0.022--0.041. Second,
dimensionality reduction always reduces PR-AUC; the magnitude of the drop
scales with the aggressiveness of the compression: 0.006 for PCA (95\%
variance retained, 18D to 13D), 0.012 for UMAP (18D to 2D), and 0.047 for
LDA (18D to 1D). Third, the payment delay features
(\texttt{Pay\_delay\_mean}, \texttt{Pay\_delay\_max}, \texttt{PAY\_0})
are the dominant predictors across all models; AGE and the education dummies
contribute negligibly.

Within the constrained AE+XGB pipeline, the ablation identifies one design
choice that matters: concatenating raw \texttt{PAY\_0} with the latent
vector costs 0.0094 PR-AUC if removed. Weighted AE loss and
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} corrections are both neutral within measurement
noise. Borderline-SMOTE on latents is harmful (PR-AUC~$= 0.4933$, the worst
single condition in the study). If the model constraint were lifted, dropping
the autoencoder and running XGBoost directly on the 18 standardised features
with a 16-cell grid search is the best-supported recommendation from
this benchmark.
